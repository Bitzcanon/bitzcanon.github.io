Soot & Scoot

Soot & Scoot is a top-down stealth game where survival becomes more difficult as your follower group expands. You take on the role of Soot, a tiny dust sprite navigating a pristine yet perilous household, hunted by relentless cleaning machines. To escape each level, you must rescue scattered Friends and guide them safely to the exit whilst avoiding the hungry gazes of Roombas looking to sweep you and your Friends up.

This game is the result of a 6-month long school project where the goal was to create a fully polished gameplay experience.

6 Programmers | 1 Designer | 1 Artist
Sep 2025 - Mar 2026 (6 Months)

Roles and Responsibilities

I lead this project as the Product Manager and Design Lead. The game idea was also initially proposed by me.

As the Product Manager, I was responsbile for delegating tasks to each team member, keeping track of deadlines, and ensuring all development materials were appropriately handled for submission.

And as the Design Lead, I was solely responsbile for creating the Unity prototype and presenting it to the team and professors. I was also responsible for fleshing out the game's design, designing each level, documenting the Game Design Document, and leading the programmers in their custom game engine implementations based off the Unity prototype.

Challenges Faced

Shaping Stealth Through Visibility

A key challenge in Soot & Scoot was balancing how much information the player should have at any moment. Stealth gameplay relies on the loop Observe → Plan → Execute, which depends heavily on player visibility.

Playtesting revealed a major issue: players were frequently caught off-guard by roaming Roombas, leading to frustration and “unfair” deaths. This shifted the experience away from intentional stealth and toward reactive gameplay.

Shown below is the original camera size used for a large chunk of development.

The root cause was a camera that was too zoomed in, limiting how much of the level players could see and preventing informed decision-making.

To address this, I redesigned both the camera system and level layout in tandem. Levels were divided into sections sized around the camera view, ensuring players could fully observe a space before acting. This restored the intended stealth loop and improved clarity.

The comparison below shows the original camera versus the final implementation.

This issue wasn't solved by tweaking isolated systems, but by reassessing the overall gameplay loop. Viewing the experience holistically made it clear that visibility was the bottleneck, leading to a solution that better supported intentional stealth gameplay.

Custom Game Engine

Another challenge faced was that this project required the development of a custom game engine tailored to the game concept. As a game designer, this meant adapting core mechanics and systems to align with engine capabilities, rather than relying on established tools like Unity.

To manage scope and technical constraints, I implemented a feature prioritization framework, categorizing systems by design impact and development complexity. Core features such as line-of-sight systems and enemy AI were identified as high priority, while lower-impact elements (e.g. particle effects) were deprioritized. This enabled effective cross-functional collaboration with the tech team and ensured development focus on critical gameplay systems.

Whilst not ideal, working within a custom engine environment strengthened my skills in systems design, technical design adaptation, and scope management. It also improved my ability to evaluate feature trade-offs, leading to more informed decisions during the game balancing and polish phases.

Toxic Tide

Toxic Tide is a 2D side-scrolling endless runner that has the player escaping a deadly wall of toxic gas. The player has to make use of their grappling hook to traverse through the obstacle-ridden laboratory, collect gears to upgrade their gear and survive for as long as they can.

This game is the result of a 3-month long school project where the goal was to create a 5-minute gameplay experience that emphasizes on aesthetics and engagement within a limited scope.

3 Designers | 2 Artists
Jan 2025 - Mar 2025 (3 Months)

Roles and Responsibilities

I served as the Technical and Gameplay Designer, responsible for the grappling hook system, camera behavior, audio integration, and data management and serialization for the leaderboard and other game data. I also coordinated the implementation of art assets from our artists.

Later, as part of an audio-focused module, I collaborated with another designer to compose and arrange background music for the Town (2nd level) and Main Menu, and sourced and edited sound effects to better suit the gameplay.

Additionally, I took on the role of Product Manager, delegating tasks, managing deadlines, and ensuring planned gameplay features were feasible within the team's scope and capabilities.

Challenges Faced

Rope Wrapping

The core mechanic of the game was the grappling hook, which was designed to feel fast, responsive, and satisfying to swing with. One of the primary challenges was ensuring that the rope itself felt like an interactive extension of the player's toolkit, rather than simply a visual line rendered between two points.

Through team discussions and prototyping, I identified that environmental interaction would be key to achieving this. I implemented a system that allowed the rope to dynamically wrap around level geometry as the player swung, preserving tension and momentum while adjusting the pivot point in real time. This significantly enhanced the sense of physicality and control.

A/B playtesting showed that players found the wrapping system more intuitive and satisfying compared to the initial fixed-anchor implementation, reinforcing the importance of environmental feedback in traversal mechanics.

Grappling While Grounded

Another key design challenge was determining how the grappling hook should behave while the player was grounded. We debated whether the hook should remain physically active, where it would apply tension and restrict movement, or whether grappling should be disabled entirely until the player was airborne.

To inform this decision, we analyzed traversal systems in games such as Just Cause and Worms, and observed player behaviour during our own playtests. Early prototypes that applied realistic tension while grounded felt mechanically consistent, but they disrupted the game's fast-paced flow and frustrated players by limiting movement at critical moments.

Given the arcade-like pacing of our game, playtesters clearly preferred responsiveness over strict physical consistency. In response, I redesigned the grounded behaviour into a hybrid solution: players could freely move without tension constraints, while still being able to fire the grappling hook and see the rope attach to surfaces. The rope remained visually present but did not apply force until the player left the ground.

This approach preserved gameplay clarity and responsiveness while maintaining visual feedback that reinforced the mechanic. The final solution struck a balance between systemic consistency and player experience, and prioritizing flow without sacrificing readability.

Undivided

Undivided is a roguelike game where you wield two forbidden masks, one of life and one of death, and shift freely between ranged and melee combat. Fight your way through a corrupted forest, collecting powerful relics that reshape your abilities with every run. Master both forms, adapt your build, and face a final boss that will test whether you were ever meant to survive this union.

This game was developed in 48 hours as part of the Global Game Jam 2026 in Singapore with the given theme of "Mask". It was also the first game jam I have participated in!

2 Developers
Feb 2026 (48 Hours)

Theme & Early Ideation

This 48-hour jam theme was "Mask".

We began with broad brainstorming rather than locking into the first idea. Our initial concepts explored themes from multiple angles, some of which included:

- A side-scroller where switching masks changed player abilities.

- A trio-based system where characters wore masks that stacked and amplified effects.

- Narrative concepts exploring identity, perspective and deception. Some also included tackling mental health issues.

- A detective story uncovering a villain represented by a recurring mask as their modus operandi

While we drawn to narrative interpretations, we ultimately chose to build a roguelike focused on mask-based combat abilities. Given the limited timeframe and our familiarity with systemic gameplay, this felt like the safest direction for delivering a complete experience. In hindsight, we prioritized familiarity over scope control, which is a valuable lesson for future jams.

Scope & Technical Direction

Choosing to make a roguelike game introduced immediate challenges. The genre is inherently content-heavy, requiring multiple enemy types, item variety, combat depth, level design and replayability.

To manage the scope, we defined a minimum playable target of one enemy type, one boss AI, two mask-based attack modes and a small pool of items with two levels. With the extra time left, we managed to expand upon this with an additional enemy type, more different items and various new level designs.

Our design goal was to ensure the mask-switching mechanic remained the core differentiator rather than building excessive content.

As development continued, the biggest challenge for this jam became the content scaling under time pressure. We had to constantly balance between the depth vs breadth of mechanics and the overall game polish vs quantity of features. Despite managing to achieve our envisioned scope, the lack of game polish made it clear that roguelikes demand significantly more content infrastructure than single-mechanic games

Key Learning Points

Scope is more important than familiarity: While we were comfortable building roguelike systems, the genre's content demands made it harder to polish individual mechanics. In future jams, I would prioritize a tighter, more mechanic-focused concept.

Modularity speeds up development: Before the jam, a tip offered by other jammers was the importance of modularity in systems, especially for projects with tight timelines. Putting it in practice, I structured the combat behaviours and enemy logic to be as modular as possible. This not only helped us iterate faster on various other types of enemies and combat, but also came with the added benefit of creating reuseable components for future projects.

Shipping over perfecting: As our first game jam, our primary goal was simply to finish and submit a playable game. Learning to make fast decisions, accept compromises, and move forward was one of the most valuable takeaways from this jam.

Groovity

Groovity is a 3D physics-based puzzle platformer inspired by Portal. Wield the Polarity Oscillation Gizmo (P.O.G) to polarize objects and solve puzzles with the power of attraction and repulsion!

This game was made during the weeklong Century Games Game Jam 2026 and won 4th place out of 25 games! The theme for the jam was "Puzzle".

4 Developers | 1 Artist
Apr 2026 (1 Week)

Theme & Early Ideation

The theme for this jam was "Puzzle".

Before the jam started, the team agreed to create a 3D game in an effort to learn and familiarize ourselves with the 3D development pipeline. With this, our plan was to adapt the theme to a 3D game regardless of what it was.

Learning from the previous jam, I proposed dedicating a full day to ideation and planning due to the longer one-week timeframe. Some initial ideas included:

- A Rube Goldberg Machine Simulator where players had to "complete the circuit" by finding placing down objects from around the room and guiding an object of interest to the finish line.

- A game where you play as a traffic police officer in Singapore, and have to conduct the traffic so that it flows smoothly. Different situations and scenarios are presented to force the player to adapt to each situation on the go.

- A puzzle platformer where the player can appoint objects as one of the magnetic poles, and making use of this to navigate through levels.

At the end of the day, we landed on the magnet-based puzzle platformer idea. We felt that this idea presented the best opportunity to explore the 3D space of game development and allow us to experiment a fully physics-based mechanic in the magnetism mechanic.

Scope & Technical Direction

We immediately recognized the parallels of this idea with a popular game called Portal, which is also a 3D puzzle platformer where the player has to "think with portals". To guide us, we took inspiration of how portal presented the player with challenges with their unique portal mechanics, which helped us think up of different interactions with our magnetism mechanic, such as allowing the player to magnetise themselves to solve puzzles.

For the game's theme, we went through different ideas that could work with the idea of magnets, and decided to try a fun and colourful theme called Space Groove, with inspiration from a skinline in League of Legends. This was where the idea slowly shifted from magnets to gravity, as it made more sense in a space-centred context.

In an effort to stay within the given timeframe, we planned the minimum playable target to include 1 tutorial level and 3 puzzle levels. Whilst development of the game mechanics were relatively smooth sailing, troubles began when it came to the level design.

The team was not too familiar with puzzle games in general, and our unfamiliarity of 3D game development caused us to struggle when coming up with compelling puzzles with the sandbox we've created. We were also unsure if we should have made the levels with the intention of a proper game progression, or create the most difficult levels we could think of to showcase what our game was fully capable of (in the context of a game jam). Ultimately, we went with the latter, which we will later learn was a mistake.

Key Learning Points

During judging, the common consensus amongst the panel and playtesters was that the game was too difficult to understand, which prevented players from fully appreciating the puzzle aspects of the game. Most players were completely stuck in the first level!

Due to the game's extensive control scheme and players getting thrown straight into the deep-end of the most difficult levels, players did not have the time to grasp gameplay mechanics and were simply too overloaded with information to solve the puzzles presented. In hindsight, our decision with how we laid out the game progression worked against us. After all, how could players begin to enjoy a puzzle without first understanding the rules?

Looking back, what we could have done to improve this was to slowly introduce mechanics one by one in separate manageable levels instead of throwing everything at players straight away in a giant tutorial followed by incredibly challenging levels. Another possible improvement was to include our control scheme as an on-screen UI, to help remind players of their possible actions.

This experience highlighted the importance of clear onboarding and thoughtful difficulty progression, especially in puzzle design.
